Week 1

Introduction to Business Management

+

What is a Business?

An organisation that provides goods and services to its customers in a specific environment. People operate businesses in order to generate profit.

Goods vs Services

  • Goods are tangible objects that satisfy needs and wants. Examples: food, clothing, furniture, cell phones.
  • Services are actions done for others. Examples: teaching, machinery repair, hair cut.

Customer vs Consumer

A customer buys products from businesses, while a consumer uses the products. You can be both at once in the same transaction.

Business Management

The art and science concerned with studying what a business is, how to establish and manage it efficiently and effectively to maximise profitability. Business management developed from economics.

Factors of Production

  • Natural resources: land, water, minerals.
  • Human resources (labour): employees with skills and expertise.
  • Capital: money or assets used to deliver value.
  • Entrepreneurship: developing and managing a venture, taking risks.
  • Knowledge: understanding your product, customers and competitors.

Profit vs Non Profit Firms

  • Profit seeking firms aim to earn more revenue than expenses. Examples: Pick n Pay, Shoprite, Spar.
  • Non profit seeking firms are need satisfying institutions. Examples: government hospitals, ministries.

Business Stakeholders

  • Business related: owners, investors, consumers, personnel, shareholders, banks, auditors, suppliers.
  • Public related: local authorities, national authorities, neighbours, NGOs, trade unions, politicians.
  • Opinion related: competitors, potential employees, media.

What is Management?

Smit and Cronje (2002): management is the process of planning, organizing, leading and controlling the resources of the organisation to achieve stated organisational goals as productively as possible.

Efficiency means doing things right, getting the most output from the least input.

Effectiveness means doing the right things, attaining organisational goals.

Week 2 and 3

Levels, Functions, Skills and Roles of Management

+

Who Are Managers?

A manager works with and through other people by coordinating their work activities to accomplish organisational goals.

Four Functions of Management

  • Planning: defining goals and strategies.
  • Organizing: determining tasks, who does them, who reports to whom.
  • Leading: motivating and influencing individuals and teams.
  • Controlling: monitoring performance against goals.

Levels of Management

  • Top managers: CEO, CFO. Organisation wide decisions and strategic direction.
  • Middle managers: department heads. Link top and first line managers, allocate resources.
  • First line managers: supervisors. Manage non managerial employees day to day.

Managerial Skills (Katz)

  • Technical skills: specialised knowledge, engineers and programmers.
  • Human skills: working with and motivating people.
  • Conceptual skills: seeing the organisation as a whole.
  • Communication skills: sending and receiving information.
  • Problem solving skills: diagnosing and solving problems.

Mintzberg's Ten Managerial Roles

  • Interpersonal: figurehead, leader, liaison.
  • Informational: monitor, disseminator, spokesperson.
  • Decisional: entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator.
Week 4

The Evolution of Management Theory

+

Classical Approaches

  • Scientific management
  • Bureaucracy
  • Administrative management
  • Human relations

Scientific Management: Frederick Taylor

Focus on maximising worker productivity. Believed there was one best way to perform a task. Introduced time and motion study, piecework, scientific selection and training.

Limitations: reduced workers to machines, assumed money is the only motivator, potential for labour exploitation.

Administrative Management: Henri Fayol

Focused on managing the whole organisation. Argued management is common to all sectors and can be learnt.

Five elements: Planning, Organizing, Commanding, Coordination, Control.

Fourteen principles include: division of work, authority, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, subordination of individual interest, remuneration, centralization, hierarchy, order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative, esprit de corps.

Bureaucracy: Max Weber

Primary focus on organisational structure. Stressed a strictly defined hierarchy governed by clearly defined goals.

Tenets: rules, specificity of competence, hierarchy, specialised training, workers do not own technology, no entitlement to position, everything written down.

Human Relations: Elton Mayo

Based on the Hawthorne Studies (1927 to 1932). Workers in relaxed, friendly groups with supportive supervisors are more productive. Social needs matter more than economic needs.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

  • Physiological: food, water, warmth, rest.
  • Safety: security.
  • Belongingness: friends, intimate relationships.
  • Esteem: prestige and accomplishment.
  • Self actualization: achieving full potential.

Theory X and Theory Y: McGregor

Theory X assumes workers dislike work and need authoritarian management.

Theory Y assumes workers see work as an opportunity to develop and take ownership.

Week 5

Contemporary Approaches to Management

+

Quantitative Management Theory

Deals with mathematical models, statistics, and other tools for decision making. Comprises Management Science and Operations Research.

Systems Approach

Regards the organisation as a system of interrelated parts. Two types: open systems which interact with the environment, and closed systems which do not.

Four parts of a system: inputs, transformation process, outputs, feedback.

Synergy means the combined effect of departments is greater than the sum of their individual effects.

Contingency Approach

Organisations are different, face different situations, and require different management. Solutions vary case by case.

Contingencies: external environment, capabilities, managers and workers, technology.

Quality Management

  • Quality control: defect identification.
  • Quality assurance: defect prevention, striving for zero defects.
  • Total Quality Management: continuous quality improvement led by top management.

W. Edwards Deming was a major proponent of TQM, known for his work in Japan.

TQM principles: listen to customers, mutually beneficial supplier relationships, manage quality, measure quality, continuous improvement, process approach, prevent problems.

Six Sigma

A vision and philosophical commitment to the highest quality and lowest cost. At Six Sigma level, only 3.4 defects per million.

DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control.

3 Sigma company: spends 15 to 25% on cost of failure, relies on inspection, believes 99% is good enough.

6 Sigma company: spends 5%, relies on capable processes, benchmarks against the best in the world, believes 99% is unacceptable.

Re-engineering

A fundamental reappraisal of how organisations operate. Focuses on cost reduction through scrutiny of people, processes, technology and infrastructure. Differs from TQM which focuses on continuous improvement.

Week 7 and 8

Organising and Staffing

+

Definition of Organizing

Henri Fayol: to organise a business means to provide it with everything useful to its functioning, including raw materials, tools, capital and personnel.

It follows planning and synchronises human, physical and financial resources.

Importance of Organizing

  • Specialization: dividing workload into activities.
  • Well defined jobs: right people in right positions.
  • Coordination: all departments working together.
  • Clarifies authority: manager's right to make decisions.
  • Effective administration: clear roles.
  • Scope for new changes: independence and growth.
  • Sense of security: clarity of powers.
  • Growth and diversification.

Process of Organizing

  • Identify activities required to achieve objectives.
  • Classify activities into groups (departmentalization).
  • Assign the group of activities to appropriate persons.
  • Delegate authority and assign responsibilities.
  • Coordinate authority: integrate all tasks and resources.

Principles of Organizing

  • Division of labour: tasks subdivided into separate jobs.
  • Departmentalization: grouping related activities into units.
  • Chain of command: line of authority from top to bottom.
  • Span of control: number of employees reporting to a manager.
  • Centralized vs decentralized authority: who makes important decisions.
  • Coordination: coordinating work of all departments.
  • Unity of command: each employee answers to one superior.
  • Responsibility: duty to perform certain work.
  • Accountability: ensuring the job is performed and giving an account.

Formal vs Informal Organization

  • Formal: structure of well defined jobs with authority and responsibility. Shown in the organisation chart.
  • Informal: network of personal and social relationships that emerges spontaneously, not based on rules.

Staffing

Koontz and O'Donnell (1995): staffing involves manning the organisational structure through proper and effective selection, appraisal and development of personnel to fill the roles designed into the structure.

Staffing process stages: estimating manpower requirements, recruitment, selection, orientation and placement, training and development, remuneration, performance evaluation, promotion and transfer.

Importance of Staffing

  • Helps discover and obtain competent employees.
  • Improves quantity and quality of output.
  • Improves job satisfaction.
  • Reduces cost of personnel.
  • Facilitates growth and diversification.

Authority and Delegation

Authority: rights within a managerial position to give orders and expect obedience.

Line authority: managers make decisions and issue orders down the chain.

Staff authority: responsibility to advise and assist others.

Delegation: assigning authority and responsibility to another person to carry out specific activities.

Advantages of Delegation

  • Frees management to concentrate on major issues.
  • Leads to better decisions.
  • Helps develop subordinates.
  • Helps in timely completion of tasks.
  • Speeds decision making.
